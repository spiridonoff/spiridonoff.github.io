# What Is Language?

*Disclaimer: I have no formal training in literature or communication. These are personal observations.*

The main task of LLMs is to predict the next token. But is that equivalent to language—or understanding it?

To me, language is simply a form of communication. We communicate to describe scenarios (real or fictional), share information, and educate others. Yet every utterance carries context beyond its literal meaning: the author's point of view, opinion, or what they want us to believe.

If a listener had a perfect language model that predicted every next word, what would be the point of the communication? If nothing is surprising, is any information left for the reader? Conversely, does failing to predict the next word mean one lacks understanding—or intelligence?

Language is hard to pin down partly because it resists a clean mathematical definition. In mathematics we simplify the world into random variables $X$ and their values $X = x_1, x_2, \ldots$, and we easily distinguish the abstract set of *variables* from their *values*. But the world contains infinitely many variables; we cannot assign a new symbol to each one.

Instead, language recycles the same set of values (words) to name the variable of interest and to express its values. That reuse—using words to define both what we are talking about and what we say about it—is what makes language tricky.

Under this view, an ideal language model would first identify the variable of interest, then predict its value. Current LLMs, which predict every token, seem to conflate both steps. Is that redundant? I'm not sure. It may still be useful when drawing conclusions: from "$a = b$" and "$b = c$", inferring "$a = c$" is a form of reasoning, even if it adds no new information. Perhaps such statements serve mainly to align understanding. Intelligent? I doubt it.
